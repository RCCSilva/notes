# Running the bases
Level: basic (sections 1-3) + intermediate (section 4, marked)
Applies to: 2026 season. Primary source S1 (Official Baseball Rules 2026). Citation: `[S1 R5.06(b)(1), p.40]` = source, rule, PDF page (printed page = PDF page - 12). S2 = 2025 rules, S3 = 2019 Umpire Manual (older, interpretations), S45-S55 = mlb.com pages (secondary).

## 1. Advancing, the force play, force out vs tag out

**Plain version.** Runners must touch first, second, third and home in that order. [S1 R5.06(b)(1), p.40] A runner owns a base once he touches it before he is out, and keeps it until he is put out or is forced to leave it for another runner. [S1 R5.06(a)(1), p.40] Two runners may not share a base: if two are touching it while the ball is alive, the following runner is out when tagged and the preceding runner keeps the base. [S1 R5.06(a)(2), p.40]

**When must a runner advance (force)?** A force play is one where a runner legally loses his right to a base because the batter became a runner. [S1 Definitions "A FORCE PLAY", p.164] Example from the glossary: the batter is forced at first whenever he puts a fair ball in play that is not caught in the air; first base has the most force plays. [S11] A runner who is not forced to leave his base is not "forced": he can stay.

**Force out vs tag out.**
- Force out: a forced runner is out if he, or the next base, is tagged before he touches the next base. [S1 R5.09(b)(6), p.59] The fielder only needs the ball in hand and to touch the base (the definition of a tag includes "touching a base with his body while holding the ball"). [S1 Definitions "A TAG", p.170] ("Force out" is not defined as a separate term; this is the reading of R5.09(b)(6) plus TAG.)
- Tag out: a runner is out if he is tagged by a fielder while the ball is alive and he is off his base. [S1 R5.09(b)(4), p.58] A tag = touching the runner with the ball (or with the hand/glove holding it), holding the ball securely. [S1 Definitions "A TAG", p.170]
- Glossary summary: the defense can retire a forced runner by tagging the next base before he arrives, but not if it first forces out a trailing runner; then the force is removed and the remaining runners must be tagged. [S11]

**"The force is removed."** [S1 R5.09(b)(6), p.59]
- If a following runner is put out on a force play, the force is removed and the runner must be tagged. [S1 R5.09(b)(6), p.59]
- The force is also removed as soon as the runner touches the base he was forced to; if he overslides or overruns it, he must be tagged. [S1 R5.09(b)(6), p.59]
- But if the forced runner, after touching the next base, retreats toward the base he had last occupied, the force is reinstated and the defense can again put him out by tagging the base he is forced to. [S1 R5.09(b)(6), p.59]

**Worked examples (rulebook).**
1. Man on first, one out, ball hit sharply to the first baseman, who touches the bag; the batter-runner is out. The force is removed at that moment and the runner going to second must be tagged. If a runner on second or third scored before the tag-out at second, the run counts. [S1 Definitions "(Force Play) Comment", p.164]
2. Same comment: had the first baseman thrown to second and the ball then been returned to first, the play at second was a force out (two outs), and the return throw to first ahead of the batter-runner would be the third out; "in that case, no run would score." [S1 Definitions "(Force Play) Comment", p.164] (A run does not score on a play where the third out is a runner forced out. [S1 R5.08(a) Exception, p.51])
3. Not a force out: one out, runners on first and third, batter flies out (two outs), the runner on third tags up and scores, the runner on first fails to get back in time and is out (three outs). This is a time play, so the run counts if the runner from third touched home before the ball was held at first. [S1 Definitions "(Force Play) Comment", p.164-165; S3 Interp. 37(10), PDF p.60 "Run counts. This is a time play, NOT a force play."]
4. Rulebook play with the force removed after oversliding: runners on first and second, before two are out, grounder; the runner from first beats the throw at second but oversliding the base, the batter-runner is out at first, the return throw tags the runner at second. "The runs score. It is not a force play. It is a tag play." [S1 R5.09(b)(6) Comment, p.59]
5. Three balls on the batter, runner on first steals, the fourth ball is thrown, and he touches second but overslides/overruns it; the catcher's throw catches him: out, because the force is removed. [S1 R5.09(b)(6) Comment, p.59]
6. Two runners touching one base when a following runner is forced to it: the following runner is entitled to the base, and the preceding runner is out if tagged or if a fielder with the ball touches the base to which that runner is forced. [S1 R5.06(b)(2), p.40-41]

**Umpire Manual (2019, S3).** I searched S3 for a separate worked "force vs tag" example and found only the time-play example above (Interp. 37(10), PDF p.60) and the statement that no run scores on a play where the third out is a force out. [S3 Interp. 37, PDF p.62] No other full worked force example was found there.

**Free advance without liability.** On a walk, hit batter or other play with the bases full that forces everyone to advance, the winning run does not count until the runner from third touches home and the batter-runner touches first. [S1 R5.08(b), p.52] (A forced runner who runs past the base he is entitled to does so at his peril; 2 out, bases full, walk, the runner from second runs past third and is tagged out by the catcher: the run still scores. [S1 R5.06(b)(3)(B) Comment, p.41])

**Open questions / UNVERIFIED (section 1).** "Force out" has no standalone definition in S1 (searched); the sentence above is a combination of R5.09(b)(6) and TAG. No rule text on "when a runner is NOT forced" other than the definition (not forced = no batter-runner pushing him; stated here only by implication of FORCE PLAY).

## 2. Stealing a base

**Plain version.** A stolen base is credited to a runner who advances one base "unaided by a hit, a putout, an error, a force-out, a fielder's choice, a passed ball, a wild pitch or a balk." [S1 R9.07, p.132] Glossary: a runner advances by taking a base to which he isn't entitled, usually while the pitcher is throwing a pitch, but also while the pitcher still has the ball or is trying a pickoff, or while the catcher returns the ball. [S48] Before 1898, any extra base (for example first to third on a single) was credited as a steal; the "modern steal rule" is from 1898. [S48]

**Is there a rule that allows/forbids stealing?** The sources never say "stealing is allowed" as a rule sentence; the rulebook treats it as a normal part of play (e.g. runners may steal on a foul tip, [S1 R5.09(b)(5) Comment, p.58]). A runner may leave the base the instant the first fielder touches a fly ball; [S1 Definitions "A CATCH", p.160] and a stolen base is a scoring term in R9.07. Leading off: the rulebook has no rule about a "lead" distance (searched "lead off"/"leading off": only batting-order uses). The glossary defines "Lead Distance" as the distance between the base and the runner's center of mass when the pitcher makes his first movement to the plate or a base. [S53] Pickoff attempts are used to keep runners close so they don't get a big lead before a steal. [S50]

**How a steal succeeds or fails.**
- Succeeds: scorer credits a stolen base under the R9.07 conditions above.
- Fails: the runner is out when tagged before reaching the base (R5.09(b)(4), [S1 p.58]); a stealing runner is "caught stealing" (CS) if he is put out, or would have been put out by errorless play, when he (1) tries to steal, (2) is picked off and tries to advance (any move toward the next base is an attempt), or (3) overslides while stealing. [S1 R9.07(h), p.134] Glossary: a caught stealing is a runner tagged out trying to steal; it can also happen before the pitch when a pickoff throw goes to first but the runner has already left for second. [S49]
- Not caught stealing: a pitched ball eludes the catcher and the runner is put out trying to advance; a base awarded for obstruction; the batter's interference out; any case where a safe runner would not have been credited with a steal. [S1 R9.07(h) Comment, p.134] Glossary adds: picked off while diving back is not CS; thrown out advancing on a wild pitch or passed ball is not CS. [S49]

**Credit rules (R9.07).** All [S1, p.132-134]:
- (a) A runner who starts before the pitch and the pitch is a wild pitch/passed ball: the runner gets the stolen base, no misplay charged (unless he advances an extra base, or another runner advances: then both are scored). [R9.07(a)]
- (b) Catcher's wild throw after the pitch: stolen base; no error unless the throw lets him take extra bases or another runner advance (then also an error to the catcher). [R9.07(b)]
- (c) A runner who evades a rundown and advances without an error gets a stolen base (also another runner who advances on that play). [R9.07(c)]
- (d) Double or triple steal where one runner is thrown out before reaching/holding his base: no other runner is credited a steal. [R9.07(d)]
- (e) Tagged out after oversliding: no stolen base. [R9.07(e)]
- (f) Safe because of a muffed throw: no stolen base; assist to the thrower, error to the one who muffed it, and "caught stealing" charged to the runner. [R9.07(f)]
- (g) Advance solely because of the defense's indifference: no stolen base, scored as a fielder's choice. The scorer considers the inning and score, whether the runner was held, pickoff attempts, whether the fielder covered the base, strategic motive. Example from the rule: runners on first and third; a stolen base should ordinarily be credited for the runner going to second if the defense legitimately chose not to contest it to keep the runner on third from scoring. [R9.07(g) and Comment]
- Glossary (secondary) versions: no steal on error; no steal if another runner is thrown out on a double steal; "defensive indifference" typically late with the defense ahead by more than one run. [S48] The glossary's "ahead by more than one run" is a typical description; the rule uses "totality of the circumstances" (rule wins).
- Official stat lines list "stolen bases" and "number of times caught stealing" for batters. [S1 R9.02(a)(9),(18), p.121-122]

**2023+ changes (as printed in glossary pages; the 2026 rulebook text for these is NOT in S1).**
- Bases 18 inches square instead of 15 ("rule change 2023"); home plate unchanged. The glossary says the change "may have a modest impact on stolen-base success rate" but its primary goal is more room and fewer collisions; it also shortens the distance between first and second, and second and third, by 4 1/2 inches, "which will likely encourage more stolen-base attempts." [S7] S1 base size: 18 in square, R2.03, p.16 (see note 01).
- Pitchers are limited to two disengagements (pickoff attempts or step-offs) per plate appearance; the limit is reset if a runner or runners advance during the plate appearance. A third disengagement that does not get the runner is a balk and the runner advances one base. Mound visits, injury timeouts and offensive timeouts do not count; an extra mound visit in the ninth inning acts as an extra disengagement. [S46; S47]
- With runners on, the timer resets if the pitcher attempts a pickoff or steps off. Timer on base: 20 seconds in 2023, reduced to 18 seconds "starting in 2024." [S47]
- What S1 does say about pitchers and runners: from the Windup Position a pitcher may deliver, step and throw to a base to pick off a runner, or disengage (step off with the pivot foot first, hands to sides) [S1 R5.07(a)(1) Comment, p.48]; he may throw to any base before his motion commits him to the pitch if he steps directly toward the base [S1 R5.07(d), p.50]; failing to step directly toward the base, or feinting a throw to first or third without completing it, is a balk [S1 R6.02(a)(2)-(3), p.91]; a snap throw followed by the step is a balk [S1 R5.07(d) Comment, p.51]. A pitcher may not disengage the rubber after every sign [S1 R5.07(a) Comment, p.47-48].

**Example (rulebook, balk is a free base).** On a balk each runner may advance one base without liability to be put out. [S1 R5.06(b)(3)(A), p.41]

**Real examples (official MLB source).**
- May 1, 1991: mlb.com's video page for the full game says Rickey Henderson "makes history on May 1, 1991 by breaking the all-time stolen base record." [S55] (Only that page description was read; no count of steals is on the page, so none is stated here.)
- A famous steal tied to the 2023 rules (Acuna): a web search found no mlb.com article page; only a video page unrelated in title. Not used.

## 3. Tag up on fly balls, overrunning first

**Plain version.** If a fair or foul fly ball is caught, a runner must be touching his original base after the catch before advancing ("tag up"); runners may leave the instant the first fielder touches the ball. [S1 Definitions "A CATCH", p.160] "Retouch" means to tag up and start from contact with the base after the catch: a runner is not permitted to take a flying start from behind his base. [S1 R5.09(c)(1) Comment, p.62]
- He is out, on appeal, if after the catch he fails to retouch his original base before he or the base is tagged. [S1 R5.09(c)(1), p.62; also R5.09(b)(5), p.58, which adds he cannot be called out for failure to retouch after the first following pitch or any play or attempted play.] It is an appeal play. [S1 R5.09(b)(5), p.58] The glossary lists "failing to tag up correctly on a caught fly ball" as a base-running appeal. [S45]
- Appeals must be made before the next pitch or any play or attempted play (at the end of a half-inning, before the defense leaves the field); an appeal is not itself a play; the appealing team must make the intent clear. [S1 R5.09(c) closing paragraphs, p.63; S45]
- No tag up on a foul tip: runners may even steal on one. [S1 R5.09(b)(5) Comment, p.58]
- 2026 new text: a runner has "passed" a base when both feet are on the ground beyond the back edge of the base (or beyond the edge in the direction he is advancing); a runner returning must retouch that base before advancing; for example, a runner who breaks from first and, returning, completely overruns first with both feet in foul territory must retouch first before again trying for second. [S1 R5.06(b)(1), p.40; Summary of 2026 changes, p.5; Appendix 6 (diagrams, listed in S1 p.6; not read in detail)]. Compare S2 R5.06(b)(1), PDF p.40, which has only the first two sentences (no definition of "passed" and no Appendix 6). S1 wins (newer).

**Example (rulebook).** One out, Jones on third, Smith on first; Brown flies out to right (two outs). Jones tags up and scores after the catch. Smith tries to return to first but the throw beats him (three outs). Jones's run counts if he touched home before the throw reached first: it is a time play, not a force play. [S1 R5.08 Comment, p.53-54]
Second example: with one out, Jones on third, Smith on second, Brown flies out to center; Jones scores after the catch and Smith scores on a bad throw. On appeal Jones is judged to have left third before the catch: three outs, no runs. [S1 R5.08 Comment, p.53] Dead-ball version: runner on second leaves too soon on a caught fly, slides into third safely, the throw goes into the dugout: he is awarded home, but while the ball is dead he must return and retouch second before touching home, or he is out on appeal. [S3 Interp. 38(4), PDF p.63]

**Overrunning first base.**
- The batter-runner cannot be tagged out after overrunning or oversliding first if he returns immediately to the base. [S1 R5.09(b)(4) Exception, p.58] Once he is back safely, he is not protected if he later loses contact. [S3 Interp. 33, PDF p.56]
- Only first base: a runner on any other base who overruns can be tagged out. The glossary and rule 5.09(b)(6) say the forced runner who overruns must be tagged. [S1 R5.09(b)(6), p.59]
- He is out on appeal if he overruns first and fails to return immediately and he or the base is tagged. [S1 R5.09(c)(3), p.62] If he attempts to run to second he is out when tagged; if he heads toward the dugout or his position and does not return "at once," he is out on appeal. [S1 R5.09(b)(11), p.61] A run scored on such a play still counts if he touched first while overrunning and was declared safe, even if he becomes the third out for not returning. [S1 R5.09(b)(11) Comment, p.61]
- Glossary on bigger bases: they could reduce over-sliding. [S7]

**Open questions / UNVERIFIED (section 3).** Appendix 6 diagrams not read in detail. Whether "tag up" appears as a defined term in the rulebook text: only in the Index ("Retouch (tag up) requirements", S1 p.189) and in the Comment to R5.09(c)(1).

## 4. INTERMEDIATE: infield fly, fair/foul fly catches, the dropped third strike

### 4a. The Infield Fly
**Exact definition.** "An INFIELD FLY is a fair fly ball (not including a line drive nor an attempted bunt) which can be caught by an infielder with ordinary effort, when first and second, or first, second and third bases are occupied, before two are out. The pitcher, catcher and any outfielder who stations himself in the infield on the play shall be considered infielders for the purpose of this rule." [S1 Definitions "An INFIELD FLY", p.166]

**Conditions (summary).** Fair fly ball; not a line drive; not an attempted bunt; an infielder could catch it with ordinary effort ("ordinary effort" = the effort of a fielder of average skill at that position [S1 Definitions "ORDINARY EFFORT", p.168]); runners on first and second, or bases loaded; fewer than two outs. [S1 p.166] (With first and third only, the rule does not apply; the definition lists only those two base situations.)

**Procedure.**
- The umpire declares "Infield Fly" immediately for the benefit of the runners; near the baselines: "Infield Fly, if Fair." [S1 p.166] The batter is out. [S1 R5.09(a)(5), p.54]
- The ball is alive; runners may advance at the risk of the ball being caught, or retouch and advance after it is touched, as on any fly ball. A foul stays a foul. [S1 p.166]
- A declared infield fly that falls untouched and bounces foul before first or third is a foul ball; one that falls outside the baseline and bounces fair before first or third is an infield fly. [S1 p.166]
- Judgment: the umpire rules whether the ball could ordinarily have been handled by an infielder, "not by some arbitrary limitation such as the grass, or the base lines"; the ruling holds even if an outfielder handles it if an infielder could as easily have. It is not an appeal play; the decision should be made immediately. [S1 (Infield Fly) Comment, p.166]
- If the infielder intentionally drops a fair ball: the infield fly rule takes precedence over R5.09(a)(12). [S1 p.166-167] (Without infield fly, an intentional drop with first, first and second, first and third or loaded and fewer than two outs makes the batter out and the ball dead; see R5.09(a)(12), p.56.)
- A runner touched by an infield fly while touching his base is not out (the batter is); touched while off his base before the ball passes an infielder, both runner and batter are out. [S1 R5.09(b)(7) and Comment, p.59-60; S3 Interp. 35, PDF p.57 (amended in 2019)]
- If interference is called during an infield fly, the ball stays alive until fair/foul is decided; if fair, the interfering runner and the batter are out; if foul, even if caught, the runner is out and the batter returns to bat. [S1 Definitions, p.167]

**Why the rule exists.** The glossary says the rule "is in place to protect against a team allowing a shallow fly ball to drop in with the intention of causing a force play at second and third or second, third and home. Otherwise, the team would be able to force out baserunners who had stayed put on a routine fly ball." [S17] The glossary also says the rule is "designed to protect baserunners." [S54] The rulebook text itself states no reason (searched the definition and comment, S1 p.166-167); it says "for the benefit of the runners." [S1 p.166]

**Worked example.** No worked numeric example for the infield fly was found in S1 or S3 beyond the interference/touch rulings above. The glossary only describes the situation in words: a shallow fly ball that the defense lets drop in, to force out runners who stayed put. [S17]

**Real game example (official MLB source).** NL Wild Card Game, Atlanta Braves vs St. Louis Cardinals, Turner Field, Friday October 5, 2012, bottom of the 8th, one out, Cardinals ahead (Braves trailing by three runs per the article): Andrelton Simmons hit a fly ball; left-field umpire Sam Holbrook called the infield fly after shortstop Pete Kozma had moved back toward the outfield grass (about 90 feet onto the grass per the article) and the ball dropped between Kozma and left fielder Matt Holliday; Simmons was out, leaving runners at second and third. Fans threw objects, causing a 19-minute delay. Braves manager Fredi Gonzalez protested the call as "not an ordinary effort"; Joe Torre disallowed the protest because it was a judgment call. Holbrook said the shortstop would have had ordinary effort and would have caught it. [S54]
Note: S54 quotes "Rule 2.00 in the definition of terms" (older numbering; the definition now sits under Definitions of Terms in S1). The text it quotes matches S1 p.166. Only this one real example found; no steal/other infield-fly play from an official MLB source found beyond S55.

### 4b. Fair vs foul, and catches
- Fair ball: a batted ball that settles on fair ground between home and first/third, or is on or over fair territory when bounding past first or third, or touches first, second or third base, or first falls on fair territory on or beyond first or third, or while over fair territory leaves the field in flight. [S1 Definitions "A FAIR BALL", p.163] Foul ball: settles on foul territory between home and first/third, or bounds past first or third on or over foul territory, or first falls on foul territory beyond first or third, or while on or over foul territory touches an umpire, player or foreign object. [S1 Definitions "A FOUL BALL", p.165]
- A fly ball is judged by the ball's position relative to the foul line (including the foul pole), not by where the fielder stands when he touches it. [S1 p.163, p.165] In contact with the ground a ball must touch fair territory, not merely be over it (new text in 2026). [S1 p.163] (Compare note 01 conflicts.)
- A fly ball that lands in the infield between home and first/third and bounces foul before passing any part of first/third is foul; one that lands on or beyond first or third and bounces foul is fair. [S1 (Fair Ball) Comment, p.163; (Foul Ball) Comment, p.165]
- A batter is out when his fair or foul fly ball (other than a foul tip) is legally caught. [S1 R5.09(a)(1), p.54] A catch is secure possession in hand or glove of a ball in flight, held firmly, without using cap, protector, pocket or uniform part; not a catch if he drops it because of a collision with a player or wall, or falling; held even if juggled if finally held before it touches the ground; he must hold it long enough to prove complete control and a voluntary release. [S1 Definitions "A CATCH", p.160] A fielder may reach over a fence/railing to catch but may not step or jump into the stands or dugout to catch. [S1 p.160] (Out-of-play steps after a legal catch: ball dead, each runner advances one base. [S1 R5.06(b)(3)(C) and Comment, p.41])
- Glossary: fair ball [S12], foul ball [S13], flyout [S27] (see note 01).

### 4c. The dropped third strike
- The batter becomes a runner when the third strike called by the umpire is not caught, if first base is unoccupied, or occupied with two outs. [S1 R5.05(a)(2), p.36]
- If first base is occupied before two are out, the batter is out even if the catcher drops it. [S1 R5.09(a)(3), p.54] "Legally caught" = in the catcher's glove before touching the ground; not legal if it lodges in clothing or paraphernalia, or if it touches the umpire and is caught on the rebound. [S1 R5.09(a)(2) Comment, p.54]
- Comment: a batter who does not realize the situation and is not running toward first is out once he leaves the dirt circle around home plate. [S1 R5.05(a)(2) Comment, p.36]
- If the batter reaches first on a dropped third strike, a wild pitch or passed ball is awarded; with a wild pitch the runner can count against the pitcher's ERA, with a passed ball he cannot. [S51 (glossary, secondary); S52]
- If the third strike lodges in the umpire's or catcher's mask or paraphernalia and remains out of play, the batter is entitled to first and runners advance one base. [S1 R5.06(c)(7) Comment, p.47]
- Umpire manual: crews should have wing umpires help on dropped third strikes near the dirt. [S3 Part I "XXIX. Dropped Third Strikes", PDF p.28] 
- Example: no worked example found in S1/S3 for the dropped third strike.

**Open questions / UNVERIFIED (section 4).** No numeric worked example for the infield fly in S1/S3. Reason for the rule only in the glossary (S17). The rulebook does not state a reason for the dropped-third-strike rule (searched, none). The one real infield-fly example is from S54 (news story, includes opinions, only judgment facts used).
